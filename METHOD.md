# How this list is built

The [README](README.md) is the answer, and this file shows the working. Every boundary drawn here is a test you can run yourself: look at a product's public pages, apply the test, and you should land where I landed.

Some of that evidence sits below the visible page. A vendor sometimes publishes an important fact without putting it on the screen: the wording is in the raw HTML, in a schema.org block (a hidden chunk of structured data describing the page), or in a JavaScript file the browser runs but never displays. Anyone can retrieve that text, though no reader will come across it by reading the page. The list uses those claims, and the README marks each one with a **†** so a reader knows to look in the source.

## The axis

**The categories are cut by who decides, not by where a tool sits in the workflow.** Knowing that a tool gets used early or late tells you almost nothing about the working relationship. Two products can both "help with the literature" while one hands you a finished review to sign and the other refuses to write a single sentence for you. What separates them is who decides what goes into the research record, and how much say the researcher keeps over it. That single question is the axis this list is cut on.

So each category answers two questions. What does the human decide? What does the AI decide? I settle both from the product's own public pages rather than from how the thing feels to use, so each boundary is written as a test a reader can run against those pages.

## The facets

The five categories are the filled-in squares of a small grid. The grid is built from five questions you can ask about any tool, and those five questions are what separate the categories from each other. Each question is what I call a facet. One word comes up in most of them: a **verdict** is the AI's call on something, such as this paper meets the criteria you set, or this is the number the study reports. Describing what a paper says is not a verdict, and neither is just putting things in order. Independence is a property questions can have, so it applies to the facets, which are close to independent. The categories are answers, a set of shelves where every tool goes on exactly one.

| Facet | What it asks | Values | Separates, in this list |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Scope** | what the AI passes a verdict on | individual items / the whole finished piece of work | Undermind from Edison Platform |
| **Standing** | what that verdict is worth if no human does anything | it is the record until someone reverses it / it waits for a person's ruling, which gets recorded / it can never be the record | Undermind from SciScore |
| **Record** | what leaves the platform as evidence of who decided | nothing / a log that puts the decision on the model / an export of the decision flow, built on rulings the platform ties to named reviewers | Consensus from Elicit Reports: nothing leaves the one, while the other exports per-step CSVs whose default entries are its own screening labels and reasoning |
| **Target** | what the verdict is about | work other people did / the researcher's own work / work not yet done | Ai2 Asta from SciScore |
| **Arbiter** | who or what settles whether the AI was right (the referee) | a reader / a run | Atinary SDLabs, listed until October 2026, from Consensus |

The last column gives, for each facet, one pair of systems that facet tells apart, which shows that every facet does work somewhere in the list. The Arbiter pair is the exception: no listed system has had a run as its referee since Bench lost its one entry in October 2026. Because each pair also differs on other facets, the list leaves open whether any single facet is indispensable.

**Span describes the systems without sorting them.** Span means how much of the research a system reaches: one step, one sub-workflow, or a question in and something you could hand in coming out. It is real and useful as a description, and it moves no entry between categories. [CONTRIBUTING.md](CONTRIBUTING.md) requires a new facet to change at least one existing placement or be turned down as decoration, and span fails that test. That result is also the cleanest evidence that the facets are doing real work: a genuinely separate dimension can be added without moving a single row.

Target does the least work inside the list and the most at its edge. All three of its values are judgments about research. There is a fourth answer, a claim about nature itself, and that fourth answer is why domain models such as AlphaFold and MatterGen fall outside the list entirely instead of into a sixth category. Their output gets checked against nature, not against anyone's standards for how the work should go.

The five categories are then positions in that grid:

| Category | Scope | Standing | Record | Target | Arbiter |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Delegation** | the whole finished piece of work | it is the record | nothing, or a log that puts the decision on the model | the brief, or material the researcher owns | a reader |
| **Standing Verdicts** | individual items | it stands until someone reverses it | nothing | work other people did, or an idea or hypothesis tested against that work or against the researcher's data | a reader |
| **Human Approval** | individual items | it waits for a person's ruling, which gets recorded | an export of the decision flow, built on rulings tied to named reviewers | work other people did | a reader |
| **Critique** | individual items | it can never be the record | nothing | the researcher's own work | a reader |
| **Bench** | a proposed condition | it waits on a commitment of materials and machine time | nothing | work not yet done | a run |

The Target cell for Standing Verdicts was widened in October 2026, when the list added entries for finding gaps, testing hypotheses and checking whether an idea is new. Patsnap's Novelty Search labels the researcher's own idea against prior patents, Yila's Research Gap and Topic Validation labels the gap a researcher's idea claims against the closest existing studies, and Ai2 Asta AutoDiscovery and HypoGeniC test hypotheses drawn from the researcher's own data against that data. The three clauses of the category's test name no target, so the tests did not change, and only the description of the category did.

One combination is impossible. A Record built on rulings tied to named reviewers only makes sense when Standing waits for a reviewer's ruling to begin with. The grid is therefore five facets that are close to independent, plus one stated rule tying two of them together.

**Bench sits outside the ranked four as the *run* answer to the Arbiter question.** Everywhere else a reader settles whether the AI was right, and at the bench a measurement does. A category needs more than a facet value to exist: its definition must also fit what this list is about. That requirement removed two former categories, each once justified as an answer to a facet. Retrieval held systems that pass a verdict on nothing, which have taken over no judgment, and Components held projects that ship no software you can run, which are not standalone systems.

Read the sections in the order they are printed and the researcher's say in the work broadly rises. The ordering is qualitative.

## The placement rules

**1. Classify separately each module that carries research judgment**, not the product as its homepage presents it. A module is one part of a product. This rule picks *which part of a product you evaluate*. When a product's modules do different jobs and leave the researcher different things to rule on, each one gets its own row and is placed by its own documentation. Ai2 Asta has one row for its literature tools and another for AutoDiscovery, which tests hypotheses drawn from the researcher's own data. Two rows of one product can therefore land in different categories. Modules that leave the researcher the same thing to rule on can share a row, as Edison's Literature and Analysis agents share the Edison Platform row. A module that carries no research judgment, or that nobody has yet tested on its own, has no row and is not shown under any job. scite is filed on its citation classification and its bibliography check, and its question-answering assistant has no row.

It cannot rescue a product whose excluded part is the one people actually buy. If the review module would qualify while the main thing the business sells is something this list excludes, the product is excluded rather than filed on its best module. A small side line in an excluded category does not trigger this exclusion. A centre of gravity in one does.

**2. Then test Bench first, and only afterwards walk Delegation, Standing Verdicts, Human Approval and Critique in order, stopping at the first match.** This picks *which category that module lands in*. The Bench test is narrow and self-evidencing, meaning a product's own pages settle it, since it wants a proposal, a run, and a human act between them. Checking it first stops one of the broader tests from catching a system it was never written for. Among the other four, the earlier category wins, because the earlier one describes the weaker position for the human, and the list should not flatter a tool.

**3. An entry can move whenever a product ships a new release.** A vendor that adds a reviewer log or a checkpoint screen moves an entry. Existing entries therefore need re-testing now and then, not just new pull requests.

Rule 2 decides which section an entry gets printed in. What describes a product is its five facet values, and the order of the tests only settles where the row appears.

## Three words the tests depend on

Several boundaries rest on these three words, so each is defined here with examples.

- **Item**: the unit the product itself invites you to accept or reject. A record in a screening set, a row in an extraction table, a citation statement, a proposed experimental condition. A passage inside a document the vendor never asks you to rule on is not an item.
- **Verdict**: a claim about an item. Whether it is fit for the purpose, how relevant it is to criteria the researcher stated, or what value can be read out of it. Describing what an item says is not a verdict, and neither is putting items in rank order. Semantic Scholar's passage labels describe. Elicit's include/exclude labels assert. scite's label on a citation statement asserts too: whether that statement supports, contrasts or only mentions the cited work, which is the judgment a researcher makes when checking whether a citation holds up. Semantic Scholar's highly-influential-citation flag marks the citations where, by the vendor's own model, the cited work had a significant impact on the citing paper, an influence label rather than a judgment about the evidence, which is why scite is listed and Semantic Scholar is not.
- **Standing**: what a verdict is worth before anyone acts on it. A verdict *stands* when it is the record that counts for that decision unless a human steps in. Undermind's label on each paper is the screening record until someone reverses it. Covidence's [suggested extraction value](https://support.covidence.org/help/settings) records nothing until a reviewer accepts it, so it does not stand.

## The tests

### Delegation

Follow the shortest path from input to output that the vendor or the repository documents. A system belongs here when three things hold. The researcher supplies a question or some source material. The next human action in the documentation is looking over a finished piece of work that would be handed in under the researcher's own name. And nothing comes out that records who decided what, unit by unit. A documented flow that instead halts for rulings the platform records against a named person, and that lets the decision flow leave as evidence, belongs in Human Approval.

### Standing Verdicts

Three clauses.

1. The system attaches a judgment to individual items: a screening label, a relevance tier, a support/contrast classification, an extracted value. A system that attaches no judgment to any item has taken over no judgment at all, so it sits outside this list.
2. That judgment **stands**. It is the record that counts for that decision unless a human steps in, rather than a suggestion that records nothing until someone accepts it. A platform where nothing advances until a named person rules belongs in Human Approval.
3. Every assertion must point back to an identifiable source a reader can open, or be explicitly marked in the output as written by the model rather than taken from a source. And the largest thing leaving the session must be structured working material or less. A document submitted under the researcher's own name belongs in Delegation.

### Human Approval

Two clauses, both required. First, the platform holds a ruling on each record that traces back to a named person, and it is that ruling, attributed to a person rather than to the model, that stands. Second, the decision flow leaves the platform as evidence: a PRISMA flow diagram (PRISMA is a reporting guideline for systematic reviews), a conflict log, an agreement report. The export need not carry names itself, since the [PRISMA diagram](https://support.covidence.org/help/export-prisma) Covidence exports during screening counts studies rather than reviewers, and its [export of individual reviewer data](https://support.covidence.org/help/export) arrives one stage later, at extraction. A flow a reader cannot rebuild at all does not count as a decision record. A platform whose labels stand before anyone rules on them belongs one category up, in Standing Verdicts.

### Critique

First, no mechanism may put AI-written text into the researcher's document or dataset unless the human acts on that specific item. A control that applies every fix at once disqualifies a tool. A tracked change the author accepts or rejects one at a time does not, because that leaves a record of exactly what was taken and who took it. Second, and separate from the first: the AI returns commentary on work the researcher has already written or recorded, rather than proposing new work or fetching someone else's.


### Bench

Three clauses, all required.

**A proposal, not a prediction.** The system must hand back a specific setup to run next: an experimental condition, a set of parameter values, a synthesis route, a machine setting. A system that hands back an estimate of what running that setup would produce, in place of the setup itself, is a domain model, and domain models fall outside this list altogether.

**A run, and the result feeds the next proposal.** The proposal has to be run and produce a number, and that number has to drive what gets proposed next. The arbiter may be an instrument or a computation, never the system's own estimate. A system that proposes once and stops fails this clause, as does one whose feedback is a reader's opinion of the proposal.

**The loop cannot advance without a human act.** Between one proposal and the next, the researcher has to be needed for something: committing the material, running the thing, bringing the measurement back. It need not be an approval control inside the software, and Atinary SDLabs documents none for its optimization loop. A default mode where the optimizer stalls until a person feeds a result back is a gate. A study that runs to the end by itself once launched is not, and neither is a setup where a robot or a script closes the loop without anyone having to do anything. Where a vendor ships both, the gate counts only if it is on by default, and the entry is re-tested when the default changes.

Software-only output is eligible. What marks this category off is that a run settles who was right, and a simulation the researcher pays for settles that as firmly as a lab assay does. The human-act clause is what keeps unattended code-running agents out of Bench, so that under [rule 2](#the-placement-rules) they go on to be tested against Delegation.

*Applied:* **Dakota** (Sandia) passes the first two clauses and fails the third: once the input deck is launched (the input deck is its configuration file), nothing waits on anyone. **Atinary SDLabs**, listed until October 2026, passed on its default mode, where the researcher runs the condition and brings the measurement back, and its public [API schema](https://api.sdlabs.atinary.com/schema/) shows what the gate really is. The loop cannot go on until you send the result back, and the API has no approve step in it: 75 paths, no accept or approve operation, no approval state among the statuses the API can report. The only approval either API documents is for the assistant's gated tool calls, such as starting a Deep Research run, and it is answered over the separate [WebSocket API](https://api.sdlabs.atinary.com/asyncapi/docs/). Its optional robot integration closes the loop without anyone having to do anything and would fail this clause, which is what rule 3 exists for. **NeuriCo**, on the HITL AutoResearch path its row names, passes the first two clauses and fails the third. Each proposal is one concrete change to an experiment, a sealed scorer scores the run, and the manager agent picks the next starting point from the scored frontier. Its docs say a legal proposal "is presented to the human for approval or concrete feedback", but both documented launch routes switch on a setting called Auto by default, under which the manager agent decides whether to admit each proposal and the human is not asked. Both points are found only in source. The gate is off by default, so NeuriCo goes on to the Delegation test.


## Where the axis coincides with the old one, and why

Two categories come close to rebuilding a section of the old arrangement, the one that sorted tools by stage of work. **Human Approval** was written against the screening platforms of the old "systematic review" section, and it has no entry at present. **Standing Verdicts** holds most of what "literature search" held, along with the tools that test an idea or a hypothesis against prior work or data. The stages the README now opens with are an index into the list and not a second set of categories: an entry's category comes from the tests above, whichever stage it serves.

In both cases there is a cause behind the overlap. PRISMA asks authors to report how many reviewers screened each record and whether they worked independently. The evidence standards that journals enforce make a trail back to named people practically necessary, so every serious screening platform converged on the same answer about who decides. Literature tools converged on labels that count unless you reverse them, and for a weaker reason: competitive pressure to put a score on everything returned. There the test did move entries. Elicit went up to Human Approval once it shipped dual review, and stayed there until the maintainer removed that row in October 2026. Semantic Scholar went in and came out again: its influential-citation classifier was first read as a verdict on each item, and later as an influence label on a citation rather than a judgment about the evidence.

Elsewhere the axis cuts hard across the old stages. Peer-review tools sort by whether the tool can write into your file, and the reports the two listed ones return write none of your text, so they sit in Critique. Bench, which took the place of the old lab section, held one proposer whose verdict comes from a run, Atinary SDLabs, with an instrument as the arbiter, and has had no entry since it left in October 2026.

- **Bench has no entry at present.** Atinary SDLabs, its one entry, left in October 2026 because its public pages offer it only through a private offer. Outerloop comes closest. - **Human Approval has no entry at present.** Its last one, Elicit's systematic-review workflow, met the test only on its dual-review path, which Elicit offers on Enterprise plans alone, and the maintainer removed it in October 2026. For Polaris and NeuriCo the facet values are clear enough on their own, but the combination lands in a cell no category currently claims: an approval on each idea or proposal the system generated, which comes before any finished work exists. NeuriCo's default setting also skips that approval, a point found only in source. Arbor idea-check returns a verdict on an idea, but neither its documentation nor its code shows its summary of prior work pointing to a source or marked as written by the model, as the third Standing Verdicts clause requires. A verdict printed in a terminal is also not finished work handed in under a name. Outerloop's pull request on each measured gain is read in its note below as a ruling on one change before it joins the base, not as finished work under the researcher's name, and the kernel's credit on that pull request is only pending until a person merges it, so it does not stand. Edison Precedent may be a fifth. Whether its cited answer on an idea's novelty counts as finished work under the researcher's name can be argued either way, and on the reading where it does not, it too is filed there by elimination. For Polaris and NeuriCo this suggests the grid lacks a cell for a person's approval of an idea the system generated, given before any finished work exists. Outerloop suggests a neighbouring gap, a person's approval of each change the system made and measured, given before the change joins the record. Arbor idea-check, and Edison Precedent on the reading where its answer is not finished work, match the widened Standing Verdicts row of the grid but fail that category's third clause, since their documentation does not show each assertion pointing to a source or marked as written by the model. Four entries now in Standing Verdicts could also be filed in Delegation by elimination on a stricter reading, as their notes below say. SCiNiTO Gap Finding Agent would move if *item* is read strictly, since its help page shows no control that accepts or rejects a gap. HypoGeniC would move if the third clause requires its hypothesis texts to cite a source or carry a mark saying the model wrote them. Patsnap Eureka Novelty Search would move if the novelty assessment its help article lists among the outputs proves to be a prose argument whose claims do not point to sources. Yila Research Gap and Topic Validation would move on either of the first two readings, since its documentation shows no control that accepts or rejects a gap status, and it says the report keeps retrieved evidence apart from inference without saying that each reframed candidate topic carries a source or a mark. OpenScholar would move to Delegation outright if a cited answer counts as finished work under the researcher's name, the reading on which Edison Platform's Literature agent, which Edison says is built with PaperQA3, and PaperQA2 sit there. It would also be filed there by elimination if the third Standing Verdicts clause is read to cover every sentence of an answer. Its paper says its outputs "may contain factual inaccuracies or unsupported information", and in its code the final check runs only when the `posthoc_at` option is set, which is off by default, and leaves a sentence that no retrieved passage supports with neither a citation nor a mark, points found only in source.

OpenResearch and NeuriCo also plan each run from earlier results on their own, but they write up what they find and sit under *Hand over the whole project*, while Outerloop hands back a pull request for each gain and a short report on each attempt. Outerloop and OpenResearch both start from a repository, choose and run their own changes and build later runs on measured gains, so what separates their jobs is the write-up alone. Bench is empty, for the reasons in the first point.

## Stages, jobs and categories

A reader arrives in the middle of a project with a job to do, such as checking whether an idea has been done or whether the citations in a draft hold up, and nobody types "Human Approval" into a search box. The README and the chooser therefore open with the stages of a project: understanding the field and the problem, finding gaps and ideas, proposing a solution and checking it is new, designing and running the experiments, and writing it up. Each stage holds one or more jobs, and each job lists every entry that does it, with what each leaves you to rule on and the category it sits in, so tools that really are alternatives to each other appear side by side. A product can appear under several stages, but only through modules that have rows of their own, so a card always shows the answerability of the module that does that job.

A system that takes a whole project to a finished write-up is listed once, under a sixth heading, *Hand over the whole project*, and is not split across the stages. It belongs there when it starts from a question, a direction, an idea or a codebase, decides which experiments or analyses to run, runs them, and hands back a finished paper or report, with no documented step at which the researcher designs the experiments. Polaris, OpenResearch and NeuriCo meet that test. Wisp Science does not, because the researcher starts each exploration and chooses which one becomes the mainline. Elicit Reports does not, because what its help article describes is a literature review, with no step that runs an experiment. Ai2 Asta AutoDiscovery chooses and runs its own analyses but hands back tested hypotheses rather than a write-up. Its package also writes a static HTML report, which its documentation presents as an offline copy of the web app's view of the run, so it is read here as a view of those results and not as a finished report. Outerloop also chooses and runs its own changes to a repository, but it hands back a pull request for each gain and a short report on each attempt rather than a write-up of the project, and a maintainer can set its next task through an issue, which its architecture notes say "always wins", so it sits under *Decide what to run next*.

The second and third stages have a bar of their own. A system that finds research gaps, proposes ideas or hypotheses, or judges whether an idea is new must leave something a reader can check, such as the papers behind each gap, a test of each hypothesis against data, a check of whether a gap is still open, or the prior work behind a novelty verdict. A generator that hands back only a ranked list of ideas is at most a close alternative, and the ones found so far are recorded in [What the tests excluded](#what-the-tests-excluded).

Within one job the categories still part company. scite and Reviewer3 both sit under checking citations. scite sorts each citation statement as supporting, contrasting or mentioning, and that label is the record unless you flag it and at least two of scite's experts agree to change it. Reviewer3's review reports on each claim and reference, and no public page describes a way for it to write into your manuscript. A reader who treats those two as interchangeable because both "check citations" is the reader this list is written for. Checking whether an idea is new splits the same way: Patsnap's label on each feature of your idea stands unless you change it, while Edison Precedent hands back one answer to keep or discard.

## Why particular entries sit where they do

Each of these is a placement that turned on one fact, recorded here so the README can stay a list.

### Delegation

**Polaris** takes a research direction to a paper and code on your own machine, and it holds the full row for that job because its documentation gives a reader the most to check. Its documentation names an approval to promote an idea, without which no experiment can be created, and a second one whenever you submit a paper in the app. It says only a person can decide either of them in the app, and that answers to the agent's questions record who answered. Before it marks a paper submitted, its own review rates every citation and checks the numbers against its run record. AutoResearchClaw does the same job and is listed as a close alternative, because its pipeline checks the paper's references against academic APIs before delivering it and drops those it judges hallucinated, while its code keeps any entry it could not check. Its gates, though, can be auto-approved or passed by another agent. Its intervention record also has no field for who acted, a point found only in source. Agentic Scientist, from Sakana AI, does the same job and is listed as a close alternative, because its paper phase audits the manuscript against the evidence from its own runs, and a git history kept beside each run commits a checkpoint after every program evaluation. By default it starts from a task that ships its own evaluator and starting program rather than from a research direction, and it marks every page of its paper "Generated by The Agentic Scientist" with no setting to turn the mark off. Its documentation describes no stop for a person once a run starts and no record of who decided, so it falls behind Polaris on the first criterion. It passes the standalone test because it starts its coding agent itself, Claude Code by default, inside its own Docker image. AI-Researcher (HKUDS), EvoScientist and NanoResearch do the same job and, on the paths they were listed for, document none of the four things the rule for end-to-end generators asks for, so they left the list in October 2026.

The same pages show that Polaris passes no category's written test. Bench fails because the rounds inside an experiment iterate with no human act until the AI decides to stop or a budget runs out. Delegation fails on its second clause, since the promotion approval is a ruling on an idea the system generated and ranked, made before any finished work exists. Standing Verdicts fails because it hands back a paper, and Critique because it writes the draft and proposes new work. Human Approval fails because most of its verdicts, on papers, ideas and citations, stand without anyone's ruling, the rulings that remain belong to whoever uses the desktop app, and no export of its decisions is documented. It is therefore filed in Delegation by elimination, as Labguru Assistant was before it left the list. Its Claude Code, Codex or Gemini CLI backends pass the standalone test because Polaris starts them itself.

**OpenResearch** serves the reader Wisp Science serves, a researcher running an agent on their own code and machine with a model they choose, and is listed for a loop Wisp does not run. It works in a Git repository, preferably an existing implementation you import, puts each hypothesis on its own git branch, runs every branch under one fixed command, freezes a branch once a run answers it, and branches the next round from the measured winner. Wisp's explorations fork sibling workspaces from one checkpoint too, but the researcher starts each one and chooses which becomes the mainline. Polaris and NeuriCo iterate on the results of their own runs as well, and both document starting from a research direction or an idea, while OpenResearch's instructions start from code and prefer an existing implementation to a blank repository. OpenResearch is filed on its default autonomy setting, Agentic. Its documentation names no default, while the code does, as does the pull request that added the setting. There its loop waits on a person between runs only when failed runs hit its repair cap, so it fails the Bench human-act clause as Dakota does. Permission prompts are a separate setting, and the source gives each coding agent its own default. It then matches Delegation: you supply the repository and a goal, and the next documented human act is reading the write-up the agent produces when it stops. NeuriCo's HITL path fails the Bench clause the same way, since its runtime holds each legal proposal for a human only when its Auto setting, on by default, is switched off, a point found only in source. Unlike OpenResearch it does not then match Delegation, because its documented plan and proposal approvals fail the second clause, and it is filed there by elimination. Neither of OpenResearch's opt-in settings holds each run for a person. Copilot asks before each run, but through an instruction added to the agent's prompt, which the pull request that added it calls "guidance to the agent, not enforcement". Claude Code's plan mode, which the documentation does not describe though the code and the pull request that re-enabled it do, holds launches until you approve a plan, once per plan rather than once per run. The entry rests on the desktop app and `orx up`, which start Claude Code, Codex, OpenCode, Cursor or Antigravity in each new chat with the user's saved settings and hand each session OpenResearch's own playbook, a step the documentation does not describe, though the code does, and the pull request that moved the playbook into `SYSTEM_PROMPT.md` describes it for `orx up` sessions. That form passes the standalone test. A second form, `orx install-skills`, only drops a skill into an agent you already run so that it can drive `orx`, and would fail that test on its own. OpenScience, a close alternative to Wisp Science, documents an Autoresearch loop too: it hill-climbs one metric, one run per idea, and records each run as kept or reverted. OpenResearch instead fixes one run command for every variant and keeps each variant on its own git branch. Every OpenScience study also has a budget agreed when it starts, a step OpenResearch does not document. OpenResearch meets the rule for end-to-end generators on its trace: every backend runs an immutable source snapshot of the recorded commit, and each run stays attached to its experiment node, so a result leads back to its run, log and code. It is listed under *Hand over the whole project* because, from the repository and the goal, it chooses each variant, runs it and writes up what it found, with no documented step at which the researcher designs the experiments.

**NeuriCo** has three ways to run. The default, Standard, runs once from idea to a paper draft with nothing waiting on anyone, and the plain AutoResearch loop runs with no human act, which is the Dakota case. Its row names the HITL AutoResearch path, which its README says "Adds a manager and human decision points to AutoResearch". Both documented launch routes switch on a setting called Auto by default, and only with Auto off does each legal proposal wait for the researcher, so the path fails Bench's human-act clause. Delegation's test reads the documented flow, while the Bench rule reads the setting a run gets. Neither is the centre of gravity, since the about page's list of what Yila does and the task buttons on its home page name literature search and review, data analysis, figures, posters, slides and typesetting and leave both out. Yila.ai also maintains an open-source skills repository whose Academic Humanizer removes "generic AI-like patterns" from academic prose. Its files call it "an editing aid, not an AI-detector bypass" and say its benchmark "intentionally does not measure detector scores", and Yila's list of agents does not include it, so it is read as a side line under the language-services rule, which says "Grammar checkers, paraphrasers and humanizers land here", rather than under the detection-evasion criterion, which every product excluded under it so far met by pitching a way past a detector. The agent itself drafts no proposal. It reads a proposal draft if one is attached, and the guide says its report "can preserve the analytical basis for proposal discussions", so the rule that excluded Science AI Journal does not reach it. The researcher writes an idea, and the guide says "After confirming the idea, click Start." Yila then decomposes the idea into a question, an intended contribution and search concepts, a step its Chinese guide calls automatic, searches for exact, adjacent, landmark, review and counterexample literature, builds a closest-work table of what each nearest study did, found and left open, looks for evidence against each candidate topic, and scores openness, contribution and feasibility from 1 to 5 in that order, stopping at the first score below 3. The idea's gap comes back with one of the four statuses its guide names, Open, Occupied, Ambiguous or Unsupported, though its use-case page gives the outcomes as "open, occupied, or under-evidenced", and each candidate gets a verdict from its scores, with the choice left "to the researcher". The about page says Yila first breaks a task into "a research plan you can confirm", but its blog promises that for complex tasks, and the gap guide shows no such step, unlike the Literature Review guide, which offers to pause on the outline.

Bench fails on its first clause, since what comes back is a verdict and candidate topics, not a setup to run. Delegation fails on its second. The next documented act is reviewing a Markdown report and a scored table "for comparing directions and discussing them with supervisors or teams", an assistive judgment the guide calls "not an exhaustive search, legal opinion, or formal novelty guarantee", and that is working material for choosing a topic rather than work handed in under the researcher's name. Standing Verdicts then matches. The status on the idea and the scores and verdict on each candidate are judgments on items, and they stand, since no documented step accepts or rejects one. For the third clause, the guide says "The report distinguishes retrieved evidence from analytical inference", and it lists the search records, the closest studies and the uncertainty among the report's contents. That is the property whose absence could move Patsnap, although here it is described for the report as a whole rather than as a mark on each assertion, and no sample report was found. The playbook's phrase for the report, a "Markdown report with citations and source markers", is wording Yila's playbooks attach to every Markdown report they produce, so it says nothing specific about this agent. What a reader can check is the prior work behind each verdict, since the report keeps the search records, the closest studies and the sources the search did not cover. The guide says Yila "does not claim novelty based only on zero results", and the Ambiguous status covers conflicting evidence or coverage "insufficient for a stable judgment", which lifts it above an idea generator that only ranks. ### Critique

**SciScore** is sold to publishers as well as to authors, and the same reports run inside submission systems. It is filed on the author path, where you sign up yourself and the report comes back to whoever wrote the manuscript.

**Reviewer3** has three paths, and the human rules on different things in each. The editor path is first-pass screening at scale. The reviewer path offers to help write a report on a manuscript someone else wrote, which would move it to [Delegation](#delegation). It is filed on the review it returns on the author path. Until October 2026 its home page stated the property that placement rests on as "We Verify. We Don't Generate." The page now says instead that "The editor can also propose revisions, but review findings are grounded in checks of the paper and its sources", and its pricing page offers "Free editing, formatting, and PDF downloads" and "AI chat and revisions across your projects" alongside the review, while its MCP page lists tools to "Edit the LaTeX manuscript" and "Submit a revision". The editor that sentence names is read as the manuscript editor these pages describe, not the editor path above. No public page says whether each revision it proposes, or each edit those tools make, waits for the author, the fact Critique's first clause turns on. The row is therefore filed on the review alone, the manuscript editor is read as a separate module with no row, and the entry is due for a re-test. A bioRxiv preprint of ReviewBench, the company's benchmark of human and AI reviews, gives Reviewer3 as the corresponding author's institution, and the site's page on the benchmark links its code but not the preprint.

**Google Deep Review** is listed as a close alternative to Reviewer3 under the manuscript job and is read the same way, on the author path. It counts as a research system although Google's announcement says its capabilities "have expanded to review documents across many domains, such as legal and finance", because it was built to review papers: the announcement calls it "the evolution of our Paper Assistant Tool (PAT)" and says it is launching publicly "Following successful deployments across 16,000+ submissions at STOC, ICML, NeurIPS, and ICLR". It passes the first Critique clause on its API reference, which says "Documents are immutable post-creation" and describes each suggested edit as "Optional suggested replacement text or edit for the anchored span". It passes the second as well, since its comparison with existing work is part of a review of the researcher's own document, as Reviewer3's checks of the literature a manuscript cites are. The same reference documents a "Structured citation verification report for a single bibliography reference", so it may also serve the citations job, which the list has not yet tested it on, and its membership is recorded under the manuscript job only.

**The closest thing this category rejects** is q.e.d Science. It cannot write into your file at all, and it still fails the second clause: it sells suggestions for experiments, and telling you what to run next is not commenting on work you have already written or recorded. It is the first rejection that clause makes on its own.

## Clusters

Some systems pass every test and still answer the same question for the same reader as one already listed: the same job, the same category, and the same thing handed in and handed back. The list groups them instead of turning them away. One featured system keeps its full row, its card on the chooser and its column in the comparison tables. Every other member of the cluster is one line under it, naming one documented difference and linking the page it rests on; scripts/lib/clusters.js keeps the exact words. When the featured system does more than one job, the cluster names the job its members are listed under, and their lines appear under that job alone. Weights you can host yourself, set against a hosted service, make a different question, as does being free where nothing comparable is. A different front end does not, and neither does a different market for the same capability.

The featured system is chosen by four criteria, in this order:

1. How clearly its public documentation states what the human rules on and what record the system leaves. That is the question this list is organised around, so it decides first.
2. How much of a README row for it a reader can verify on public pages.
3. Backing and maintenance: an institution, company or publication behind it, recent releases, and more than one substantial contributor.
4. Adoption, in stars or users, only to break a tie.

When a system reached the list counts for nothing. When the weekly scan finds a new member for a cluster, it compares the newcomer with the featured system on these criteria in both directions and recommends. The maintainer decides, and a featured system that loses becomes one line in its own cluster. A system that answers a question no listed system answers still gets a row of its own.

- **Polaris** is featured for *direction in, paper and code out, on your machine*, ahead of AutoResearchClaw and Agentic Scientist (Sakana AI). Criterion 1. Its docs say only a person can decide a gate in the app, and the topic owner rules on idea promotion. AutoResearchClaw's stage gates can be auto-approved, which its quick start does, or approved by another agent. Its intervention record has no field for who acted, a point found only in source. Good work, but a curated list should not point a researcher at software whose upkeep depends on one person staying interested. |
| [scientific-agent-skills](https://github.com/k-dense-ai/scientific-agent-skills) | A library of skills with no software of its own to run them. Whatever agent loads it sets the permissions and the checkpoints, so it is not a standalone research system. The exclusion concerns what it is, not how good it is: it has 45,000+ stars and sixteen contributors. |
| [q.e.d Science](https://qedscience.com/) | No write path at all, and it still fails Critique. It sells experimental proposals, and commentary telling you what to run next is not commentary on work already recorded. The only entry so far rejected by that clause alone. |
| [Dakota](https://dakota.sandia.gov/) | The textbook computational loop, and no gate. Once the input deck is launched it runs to termination with nothing waiting on a researcher. Excluded on the human-act clause, not on the Arbiter facet. |
| [statcheck](http://statcheck.io/) | Recomputes the statistics a paper reports and flags the ones that do not add up, with no write path, which is exactly the Critique shape. Excluded because it is deterministic parsing and arithmetic, meaning the same paper always gives the same answer, with no model in it, and this list describes itself in model terms from its first paragraph on. |
| Domain models: [AlphaFold](https://alphafoldserver.com/), [Boltz](https://github.com/jwohlwend/boltz), [MatterGen](https://github.com/microsoft/mattergen), ML weather emulators | Their output is a claim about nature, checked against nature. They substitute for a measurement rather than for a judgment about how the research should go. Several are among the most consequential scientific software ever released, and the exclusion is one of scope alone. |
| Prompt-template "hypothesis generators" | A prompt wrapped in a landing page is not a system. |
| Individual papers and preprints | Not in this list. See [Related lists](#related-lists) below. |

If a system here becomes generally available, or ships the module that would change where it sits, open a PR.

## Related lists

For the academic-survey side of this space, which changes fast and is better served by lists dedicated to tracking papers:

- [Awesome-AI-Scientists](https://github.com/tsinghua-fib-lab/Awesome-AI-Scientists): closest to HAIRS in spirit, and it also covers modes where the human is in or out of the loop.
- [awesome-ai-for-science](https://github.com/yenanjing/awesome-ai-for-science): 400+ open-source projects, code only, no commercial products.
- [Awesome-LLM-Scientific-Discovery](https://github.com/HKUST-KnowComp/Awesome-LLM-Scientific-Discovery): the paper list that accompanies an EMNLP 2025 survey.

Overlapping less directly: [Awesome-Agent-Scientists](https://github.com/AgenticScience/Awesome-Agent-Scientists), [Awesome-LLM-Agents-Scientific-Discovery](https://github.com/zjlrock777/Awesome-LLM-Agents-Scientific-Discovery) (biomedical), [awesome-deep-research-agent](https://github.com/WuizaKaseiyo/awesome-deep-research-agent) (general-purpose), [awesome-HAI](https://github.com/bwang514/awesome-HAI) (Human-AI *Interaction* papers).

This list differs from all of them in two ways: its primary focus is live commercial products, and it organizes its entries by who decides what enters the research record.

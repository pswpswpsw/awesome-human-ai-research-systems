# How this list is built

The [README](README.md) is the answer, and this file shows the working. Every boundary drawn here is a test you can run yourself: look at a product's public pages, apply the test, and you should land where I landed.

Some of that evidence sits below the visible page. A vendor sometimes publishes an important fact without putting it on the screen: the wording is in the raw HTML, in a schema.org block (a hidden chunk of structured data describing the page), or in a JavaScript file the browser runs but never displays. Anyone can retrieve that text, though no reader will come across it by reading the page. The list uses those claims, and the README marks each one with a **†** so a reader knows to look in the source.

## The axis

**The categories are cut by who decides, not by where a tool sits in the workflow.** Knowing that a tool gets used early or late tells you almost nothing about the working relationship. Two products can both "help with the literature" while one hands you a finished review to sign and the other refuses to write a single sentence for you. What separates them is who decides what goes into the research record, and how much say the researcher keeps over it. That single question is the axis this list is cut on.

So each category answers two questions. What does the human decide? What does the AI decide? I settle both from the product's own public pages rather than from how the thing feels to use, so each boundary is written as a test a reader can run against those pages.

## The facets

The five categories are the filled-in squares of a small grid. The grid is built from five questions you can ask about any tool, and those five questions are what separate the categories from each other. Each question is what I call a facet. One word comes up in most of them: a **verdict** is the AI's call on something, such as this paper meets the criteria you set, or this is the number the study reports. Describing what a paper says is not a verdict, and neither is just putting things in order. Independence is a property questions can have, so it applies to the facets, which are close to independent. The categories are answers, a set of shelves where every tool goes on exactly one.

| Facet | What it asks | Values | Separates, in this list |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Scope** | what the AI passes a verdict on | individual items / the whole finished piece of work | Undermind from Edison Platform |
| **Standing** | what that verdict is worth if no human does anything | it is the record until someone reverses it / it waits for a person's ruling, which gets recorded / it can never be the record | Undermind from Reviewer3 |
| **Record** | what leaves the platform as evidence of who decided | nothing / a log that puts the decision on the model / an export of the decision flow, built on rulings the platform ties to named reviewers | Consensus from Elicit: nothing leaves the one, while the other logs every decision, override and adjudication and exports a PRISMA flow diagram |
| **Target** | what the verdict is about | work other people did / the researcher's own work / work not yet done | Elicit from SciScore |
| **Arbiter** | who or what settles whether the AI was right (the referee) | a reader / a run | Atinary from Consensus |

The last column gives, for each facet, one pair of systems that facet tells apart, which shows that every facet does work somewhere in the list. Because each pair also differs on other facets, the list leaves open whether any single facet is indispensable.

**Span describes the systems without sorting them.** Span means how much of the research a system reaches: one step, one sub-workflow, or a question in and something you could hand in coming out. It is real and useful as a description, and it moves no entry between categories. [CONTRIBUTING.md](CONTRIBUTING.md) requires a new facet to change at least one existing placement or be turned down as decoration, and span fails that test. That result is also the cleanest evidence that the facets are doing real work: a genuinely separate dimension can be added without moving a single row.

Target does the least work inside the list and the most at its edge. All three of its values are judgments about research. There is a fourth answer, a claim about nature itself, and that fourth answer is why domain models such as AlphaFold and MatterGen fall outside the list entirely instead of into a sixth category. Their output gets checked against nature, not against anyone's standards for how the work should go.

The five categories are then positions in that grid:

| Category | Scope | Standing | Record | Target | Arbiter |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Delegation** | the whole finished piece of work | it is the record | nothing, or a log that puts the decision on the model | the brief, or material the researcher owns | a reader |
| **Standing Verdicts** | individual items | it stands until someone reverses it | nothing | work other people did, or an idea or hypothesis tested against that work or against the researcher's data | a reader |
| **Human Approval** | individual items | it waits for a person's ruling, which gets recorded | an export of the decision flow, built on rulings tied to named reviewers | work other people did | a reader |
| **Critique** | individual items | it can never be the record | nothing | the researcher's own work | a reader |
| **Bench** | a proposed condition | it waits on a commitment of materials and machine time | nothing | work not yet done | a run |

The Target cell for Standing Verdicts was widened in October 2026, when the list added entries for finding gaps, testing hypotheses and checking whether an idea is new. Patsnap's Novelty Search labels the researcher's own idea against prior patents, and Ai2 Asta AutoDiscovery and HypoGeniC test hypotheses drawn from the researcher's own data against that data. The three clauses of the category's test name no target, so the tests did not change, and only the description of the category did.

One combination is impossible. A Record built on rulings tied to named reviewers only makes sense when Standing waits for a reviewer's ruling to begin with. The grid is therefore five facets that are close to independent, plus one stated rule tying two of them together.

**Bench sits outside the ranked four as the *run* answer to the Arbiter question.** Everywhere else a reader settles whether the AI was right, and at the bench a measurement does. A category needs more than a facet value to exist: its definition must also fit what this list is about. That requirement removed two former categories, each once justified as an answer to a facet. Retrieval held systems that pass a verdict on nothing, which have taken over no judgment, and Components held projects that ship no software you can run, which are not standalone systems.

Read the sections in the order they are printed and the researcher's say in the work broadly rises. The ordering is qualitative.

## The placement rules

**1. Classify separately each module that carries research judgment**, not the product as its homepage presents it. A module is one part of a product. This rule picks *which part of a product you evaluate*. When a product's modules do different jobs and leave the researcher different things to rule on, each one gets its own row and is placed by its own documentation, so one product can sit in two categories. Elicit's systematic-review workflow sits in Human Approval on its dual-review path, while Elicit Reports, which writes a report on its own search and screening, sits in Delegation. Modules that leave the researcher the same thing to rule on can share a row, as Edison's Literature and Analysis agents share the Edison Platform row. A module that carries no research judgment, or that nobody has yet tested on its own, has no row and is not shown under any job. scite is filed on its citation classification and its bibliography check, and its question-answering assistant has no row.

It cannot rescue a product whose excluded part is the one people actually buy. If the review module would qualify while the main thing the business sells is something this list excludes, the product is excluded rather than filed on its best module. A small side line in an excluded category does not trigger this exclusion. A centre of gravity in one does.

**2. Then test Bench first, and only afterwards walk Delegation, Standing Verdicts, Human Approval and Critique in order, stopping at the first match.** This picks *which category that module lands in*. The Bench test is narrow and self-evidencing, meaning a product's own pages settle it, since it wants a proposal, a run, and a human act between them. Checking it first stops one of the broader tests from catching a system it was never written for. Among the other four, the earlier category wins, because the earlier one describes the weaker position for the human, and the list should not flatter a tool.

**3. An entry can move whenever a product ships a new release.** A vendor that adds a reviewer log or a checkpoint screen moves an entry. Existing entries therefore need re-testing now and then, not just new pull requests.

Rule 2 decides which section an entry gets printed in. What describes a product is its five facet values, and the order of the tests only settles where the row appears.

## Three words the tests depend on

Several boundaries rest on these three words, so each is defined here with examples.

- **Item**: the unit the product itself invites you to accept or reject. A record in a screening set, a row in an extraction table, a citation statement, a proposed experimental condition. A passage inside a document the vendor never asks you to rule on is not an item.
- **Verdict**: a claim about an item. Whether it is fit for the purpose, how relevant it is to criteria the researcher stated, or what value can be read out of it. Describing what an item says is not a verdict, and neither is putting items in rank order. Semantic Scholar's passage labels describe. Elicit's include/exclude labels assert. scite's label on a citation statement asserts too: whether that statement supports, contrasts or only mentions the cited work, which is the judgment a researcher makes when checking whether a citation holds up. Semantic Scholar's highly-influential-citation flag marks the citations where, by the vendor's own model, the cited work had a significant impact on the citing paper, an influence label rather than a judgment about the evidence, which is why scite is listed and Semantic Scholar is not.
- **Standing**: what a verdict is worth before anyone acts on it. A verdict *stands* when it is the record that counts for that decision unless a human steps in. Undermind's label on each paper is the screening record until someone reverses it. Covidence's suggested extraction value records nothing until a reviewer accepts it, so it does not stand.

## The tests

### Delegation

Follow the shortest path from input to output that the vendor or the repository documents. A system belongs here when three things hold. The researcher supplies a question or some source material. The next human action in the documentation is looking over a finished piece of work that would be handed in under the researcher's own name. And nothing comes out that records who decided what, unit by unit. A documented flow that instead halts for rulings the platform records against a named person, and that lets the decision flow leave as evidence, belongs in Human Approval.

### Standing Verdicts

Three clauses.

1. The system attaches a judgment to individual items: a screening label, a relevance tier, a support/contrast classification, an extracted value. A system that attaches no judgment to any item has taken over no judgment at all, so it sits outside this list.
2. That judgment **stands**. It is the record that counts for that decision unless a human steps in, rather than a suggestion that records nothing until someone accepts it. A platform where nothing advances until a named person rules belongs in Human Approval.
3. Every assertion must point back to an identifiable source a reader can open, or be explicitly marked in the output as written by the model rather than taken from a source. And the largest thing leaving the session must be structured working material or less. A document submitted under the researcher's own name belongs in Delegation.

### Human Approval

Two clauses, both required. First, the platform holds a ruling on each record that traces back to a named person, and it is that ruling, attributed to a person rather than to the model, that stands. Second, the decision flow leaves the platform as evidence: a PRISMA flow diagram (PRISMA is a reporting guideline for systematic reviews), a conflict log, an agreement report. The export need not carry names itself, since Covidence's screening-stage PRISMA does not and its export naming each reviewer arrives one stage later. A flow a reader cannot rebuild at all does not count as a decision record. A platform whose labels stand before anyone rules on them belongs one category up, in Standing Verdicts.

### Critique

First, no mechanism may put AI-written text into the researcher's document or dataset unless the human acts on that specific item. A control that applies every fix at once disqualifies a tool. A tracked change the author accepts or rejects one at a time does not, because that leaves a record of exactly what was taken and who took it. Second, and separate from the first: the AI returns commentary on work the researcher has already written or recorded, rather than proposing new work or fetching someone else's.


### Bench

Three clauses, all required.

**A proposal, not a prediction.** The system must hand back a specific setup to run next: an experimental condition, a set of parameter values, a synthesis route, a machine setting. A system that hands back an estimate of what running that setup would produce, in place of the setup itself, is a domain model, and domain models fall outside this list altogether.

**A run, and the result feeds the next proposal.** The proposal has to be run and produce a number, and that number has to drive what gets proposed next. The arbiter may be an instrument or a computation, never the system's own estimate. A system that proposes once and stops fails this clause, as does one whose feedback is a reader's opinion of the proposal.

**The loop cannot advance without a human act.** Between one proposal and the next, the researcher has to be needed for something: committing the material, running the thing, bringing the measurement back. It need not be an approval control inside the software, and Atinary ships none. A default mode where the optimizer stalls until a person feeds a result back is a gate. A study that runs to the end by itself once launched is not, and neither is a setup where a robot or a script closes the loop without anyone having to do anything. Where a vendor ships both, the gate counts only if it is on by default, and the entry is re-tested when the default changes.

Software-only output is eligible. What marks this category off is that a run settles who was right, and a simulation the researcher pays for settles that as firmly as a lab assay does. The human-act clause is what keeps unattended code-running agents out of Bench, so that under [rule 2](#the-placement-rules) they go on to be tested against Delegation.

*Applied:* **Dakota** (Sandia) passes the first two clauses and fails the third: once the input deck is launched (the input deck is its configuration file), nothing waits on anyone. **Atinary** passes on its default mode, where the researcher runs the condition and brings the measurement back, and a [trace of its API](WORKFLOWS.md) shows what the gate really is. The loop cannot go on until you send the result back, and the API has no approve step in it: 95 paths, no accept or approve operation, no approval state among the statuses the API can report. Its optional robot integration closes the loop without anyone having to do anything and would fail this clause, which is what rule 3 exists for. **NeuriCo**, on the HITL AutoResearch path its row names, passes the first two clauses and fails the third. Each proposal is one concrete change to an experiment, a sealed scorer scores the run, and the manager agent picks the next starting point from the scored frontier. Its docs say a legal proposal "is presented to the human for approval or concrete feedback", but both documented launch routes switch on a setting called Auto by default, under which the manager agent decides whether to admit each proposal and the human is not asked. Both points are found only in source. The gate is off by default, so NeuriCo goes on to the Delegation test.


## Where the axis coincides with the old one, and why

Two categories come close to rebuilding a section of the old arrangement, the one that sorted tools by stage of work. **Human Approval** holds exactly the membership that "systematic review" held, and **Standing Verdicts** holds most of what "literature search" held, along with the tools that test an idea or a hypothesis against prior work or data. The stages the README now opens with are an index into the list and not a second set of categories: an entry's category comes from the tests above, whichever stage it serves.

In both cases there is a cause behind the overlap. PRISMA asks authors to report how many reviewers screened each record and whether they worked independently. The evidence standards that journals enforce make a trail back to named people practically necessary, so every serious screening platform converged on the same answer about who decides. Literature tools converged on labels that count unless you reverse them, and for a weaker reason: competitive pressure to put a score on everything returned. There the test did move entries. Elicit went up to Human Approval once it shipped dual review. Semantic Scholar went in and came out again: its influential-citation classifier was first read as a verdict on each item, and later as an influence label on a citation rather than a judgment about the evidence.

Elsewhere the axis cuts hard across the old stages. Peer-review tools sort by whether the tool can write into your file, and the two listed ones write none of your text, so they sit in Critique. Bench, which took the place of the old lab section, now holds one proposer whose verdict comes from a run: Atinary, with an instrument as the arbiter.

## Known weak points

- **Bench's computational cell is empty.** Software-only output is eligible, but every computational loop found so far either needs no human act at its default settings (Dakota, ShinkaEvolve, OpenResearch, and NeuriCo, whose manager agent decides on each proposal unless its Auto setting, on by default, is switched off, a point found only in source) or is a library rather than a system you can run on its own (BoTorch, Ax, Xopt).
- **Critique covers verification only.** Its two entries return verification reports, and the product I found that critiques the argument itself, q.e.d Science, crosses over into proposing new experiments and fails the second clause.
- **Polaris, NeuriCo and Arbor idea-check satisfy no category's written test** and are filed in Delegation by elimination, the precedent Labguru Assistant set before it left the list. For Polaris and NeuriCo the facet values are clear enough on their own, but the combination lands in a cell no category currently claims: an approval on each idea or proposal the system generated, which comes before any finished work exists. NeuriCo's default setting also skips that approval, a point found only in source. Arbor idea-check returns a verdict on an idea, but neither its documentation nor its code shows its summary of prior work pointing to a source or marked as written by the model, as the third Standing Verdicts clause requires. A verdict printed in a terminal is also not finished work handed in under a name. Edison Precedent may be a fourth. Whether its cited answer on an idea's novelty counts as finished work under the researcher's name can be argued either way, and on the reading where it does not, it too is filed there by elimination. For Polaris and NeuriCo this suggests the grid lacks a cell for a person's approval of an idea the system generated, given before any finished work exists. Arbor idea-check, and Edison Precedent on the reading where its answer is not finished work, match the widened Standing Verdicts row of the grid but fail that category's third clause, since their documentation does not show each assertion pointing to a source or marked as written by the model. Three entries now in Standing Verdicts could also be filed in Delegation by elimination on a stricter reading, as their notes below say. SCiNiTO Gap Finding Agent would move if *item* is read strictly, since its help page shows no control that accepts or rejects a gap. HypoGeniC would move if the third clause requires its hypothesis texts to cite a source or carry a mark saying the model wrote them. Patsnap Eureka Novelty Search would move if the novelty assessment its help article lists among the outputs proves to be a prose argument whose claims do not point to sources.
- **The lab job holds one system.** *Decide what to run next in the lab* lists Atinary alone, now that OpenResearch and NeuriCo, which plan each run from earlier results on their own and write up what they find, sit under *Hand over the whole project*. Bench holds Atinary alone for the reason in the first point.

## Stages, jobs and categories

A reader arrives in the middle of a project with a job to do, such as checking whether an idea has been done or whether the citations in a draft hold up, and nobody types "Human Approval" into a search box. The README and the chooser therefore open with the stages of a project: understanding the field and the problem, finding gaps and ideas, proposing a solution and checking it is new, designing and running the experiments, and writing it up. Each stage holds one or more jobs, and each job lists every entry that does it, with what each leaves you to rule on and the category it sits in, so tools that really are alternatives to each other appear side by side. A product can appear under several stages, but only through modules that have rows of their own, so a card always shows the answerability of the module that does that job.

A system that takes a whole project to a finished write-up is listed once, under a sixth heading, *Hand over the whole project*, and is not split across the stages. It belongs there when it starts from a question, a direction, an idea or a codebase, decides which experiments or analyses to run, runs them, and hands back a finished paper or report, with no documented step at which the researcher designs the experiments. Polaris, OpenResearch and NeuriCo meet that test. Wisp Science does not, because the researcher starts each exploration and chooses which one becomes the mainline. Elicit Reports does not, because what its help article describes is a literature review, with no step that runs an experiment. Ai2 Asta AutoDiscovery chooses and runs its own analyses but hands back tested hypotheses rather than a write-up. Its package also writes a static HTML report, which its documentation presents as an offline copy of the web app's view of the run, so it is read here as a view of those results and not as a finished report.

The second and third stages have a bar of their own. A system that finds research gaps, proposes ideas or hypotheses, or judges whether an idea is new must leave something a reader can check, such as the papers behind each gap, a test of each hypothesis against data, a check of whether a gap is still open, or the prior work behind a novelty verdict. A generator that hands back only a ranked list of ideas is at most a close alternative, and the ones found so far are recorded in [What the tests excluded](#what-the-tests-excluded).

Within one job the categories still part company. scite and Reviewer3 both sit under checking citations. scite sorts each citation statement as supporting, contrasting or mentioning, and that label is the record unless you open the source and reverse it. Reviewer3 reports on each claim and reference, and nothing it writes reaches your manuscript unless you act on that finding. A reader who treats those two as interchangeable because both "check citations" is the reader this list is written for. Checking whether an idea is new splits the same way: Patsnap's label on each feature of your idea stands unless you change it, while Edison Precedent hands back one answer to keep or discard.

## Why particular entries sit where they do

Each of these is a placement that turned on one fact, recorded here so the README can stay a list.

### Delegation

**Polaris** takes a research direction to a paper and code on your own machine, and it holds the full row for that job because its documentation gives a reader the most to check. Its documentation names an approval to promote an idea, without which no experiment can be created, and a second one whenever you submit a paper in the app. It says only a person can decide either of them in the app, and that answers to the agent's questions record who answered. Before it marks a paper submitted, its own review rates every citation and checks the numbers against its run record. AutoResearchClaw does the same job and is listed as a close alternative, because its pipeline verifies every reference against real APIs before it delivers the paper. Its gates, though, can be auto-approved or passed by another agent. Its intervention record also has no field for who acted, a point found only in source. AI-Researcher (HKUDS), EvoScientist and NanoResearch do the same job and, on the paths they were listed for, document none of the four things the rule for end-to-end generators asks for, so they left the list in October 2026.

The same pages show that Polaris passes no category's written test. Bench fails because the rounds inside an experiment iterate with no human act until the AI decides to stop or a budget runs out. Delegation fails on its second clause, since the promotion approval is a ruling on an idea the system generated and ranked, made before any finished work exists. Standing Verdicts fails because it hands back a paper, and Critique because it writes the draft and proposes new work. Human Approval fails because most of its verdicts, on papers, ideas and citations, stand without anyone's ruling, the rulings that remain belong to whoever uses the desktop app, and no export of its decisions is documented. It is therefore filed in Delegation by elimination, as Labguru Assistant was before it left the list. Its Claude Code, Codex or Gemini CLI backends pass the standalone test because Polaris starts them itself.

**OpenResearch** serves the reader Wisp Science serves, a researcher running an agent on their own code and machine with a model they choose, and is listed for a loop Wisp does not run. It works in a Git repository, preferably an existing implementation you import, puts each hypothesis on its own git branch, runs every branch under one fixed command, freezes a branch once a run answers it, and branches the next round from the measured winner. Wisp's explorations fork sibling workspaces from one checkpoint too, but the researcher starts each one and chooses which becomes the mainline. Polaris and NeuriCo iterate on the results of their own runs as well, and both document starting from a research direction or an idea, while OpenResearch's instructions start from code and prefer an existing implementation to a blank repository. OpenResearch is filed on its default autonomy setting, Agentic. Its documentation names no default, while the code does, as does the pull request that added the setting. There its loop waits on a person between runs only when failed runs hit its repair cap, so it fails the Bench human-act clause as Dakota does. Permission prompts are a separate setting, and the source gives each coding agent its own default. It then matches Delegation: you supply the repository and a goal, and the next documented human act is reading the write-up the agent produces when it stops. NeuriCo's HITL path fails the Bench clause the same way, since its runtime holds each legal proposal for a human only when its Auto setting, on by default, is switched off, a point found only in source. Unlike OpenResearch it does not then match Delegation, because its documented plan and proposal approvals fail the second clause, and it is filed there by elimination. Neither of OpenResearch's opt-in settings holds each run for a person. Copilot asks before each run, but through an instruction added to the agent's prompt, which the pull request that added it calls "guidance to the agent, not enforcement". Claude Code's plan mode, found only in source, holds launches until you approve a plan, once per plan rather than once per run. The entry rests on the desktop app and `orx up`, which start Claude Code, Codex, OpenCode, Cursor or Antigravity in each new chat with the user's saved settings and hand each session OpenResearch's own playbook, a step only the source shows. That form passes the standalone test. A second form, `orx install-skills`, only drops a skill into an agent you already run so that it can drive `orx`, and would fail that test on its own. OpenScience, a close alternative to Wisp Science, documents an Autoresearch loop too: it hill-climbs one metric, one run per idea, and records each run as kept or reverted. OpenResearch instead fixes one run command for every variant and keeps each variant on its own git branch. Every OpenScience study also has a budget agreed when it starts, a step OpenResearch does not document. OpenResearch meets the rule for end-to-end generators on its trace: every backend runs an immutable source snapshot of the recorded commit, and each run stays attached to its experiment node, so a result leads back to its run, log and code. It is listed under *Hand over the whole project* because, from the repository and the goal, it chooses each variant, runs it and writes up what it found, with no documented step at which the researcher designs the experiments.

**NeuriCo** has three ways to run. The default, Standard, runs once from idea to a paper draft with nothing waiting on anyone, and the plain AutoResearch loop runs with no human act, which is the Dakota case. Its row names the HITL AutoResearch path, which its README says "Adds a manager and human decision points to AutoResearch". Both documented launch routes switch on a setting called Auto by default, and only with Auto off does each legal proposal wait for the researcher, so the path fails Bench's human-act clause. Delegation's test reads the documented flow, while the Bench rule reads the setting a run gets. The documented flow gives the human the plan and proposal approvals, which fail Delegation's second clause in the way Polaris's promotion approval does, though Polaris's is always on. NeuriCo, like Polaris, therefore satisfies no category's written test and is filed in Delegation by elimination. Under Auto the manager agent makes those approvals and is not allowed to ask the human, so in practice the next human act is reading the scored results and the paper it hands back, and its idea log can record a decision only as the worker's or the manager's, naming no person. The Auto default and both of these behaviours are found only in source. As a Delegation entry it meets the rule for end-to-end generators on its trace: the runtime checkpoints the exact reviewed workspace and runs its sealed scorer against that checkpoint, so each scored result leads back to its code. The trace covers the scored results. The paper, which both launch routes write unless you switch it off at launch, is drafted from the experiment report with no documented link back to the runs, and that default is also found only in source. On this path its workers can only be Claude Code or Codex, because both launch routes refuse any other provider, another point found only in source. NeuriCo passes the standalone test only because it starts those workers inside its own runtime. It is listed under *Hand over the whole project*: from the idea, its workers propose and run each change, and on the default Auto setting the manager agent rather than the researcher decides which proposals go ahead, before the paper is drafted. It shares that heading and its category with Polaris and keeps a row of its own because what it is handed differs. Polaris takes a research direction and generates and ranks its own ideas before one is promoted, while NeuriCo takes one idea the researcher writes, of which its idea guide says "Only `title`, `domain`, and `hypothesis` are required."

**Edison Platform** is filed on its Literature and Analysis agents, which Edison's May 2026 announcement says "will remain publicly available through the Edison Playground". Until October 2026 the row also covered Kosmos, which moved to access by application in May 2026 and is recorded in [What the tests excluded](#what-the-tests-excluded), and Edison's narrower agents, among them Precedent, which now has a row of its own. Literature answers a question from the literature and Analysis answers one from files you upload, and both hand back an answer to keep, revise or discard, so they leave the researcher the same thing to rule on and share one row. It meets the rule for end-to-end generators on its trace: through the API, when a task is fetched with the verbose setting, a Literature answer comes with the contexts and paper metadata behind it, and an Analysis task leaves the notebook in which its analysis code was written. That setting changes what the client downloads, not what the run does.

**Edison Precedent** is the Edison Platform agent that answers whether an idea has been tried before. It has a row of its own because it serves the novelty job, and what it does and hands back differ from what the Edison Platform row describes. You put the idea as a "Has anyone" question and read one answer with references. API tasks are, in the vendor's words, "fire-and-forget, not interactive", and no checkpoint inside a run is documented. Bench fails on its first clause, since its docs describe no setup for you to run. Delegation is the close call. You supply a question, no documented output records who decided what, and the next documented act is reading a finished answer. Whether a novelty answer counts as work handed in under your own name can be argued either way, so the placement does not rest on that reading alone. Read the other way, the module satisfies no other category's written test. Its docs describe a judgment on the idea itself, which would satisfy the first Standing Verdicts clause as it does for Patsnap, and that judgment stands. The docs, though, promise an answer "with references" rather than saying that every assertion points to a source or is marked as model text, so the third clause fails. OpenScholar, whose answers tie each claim to a source, passes that clause. Precedent's docs tie no ruling to a named person, so Human Approval fails too. Critique fails because what Precedent judges is an idea for work not yet done, which its docs illustrate with a check run "Before starting SSR1 knock-in experiments", rather than work the researcher has already written or recorded. It would then be filed in Delegation by elimination. What a reader can check is the references behind the answer, and the API's `has_successful_answer` flag says whether the agent found a good answer. The web app turns a false flag into the notice "No answer could be found among the sources." and shows a Yes or No badge taken from the first yes or no word in the answer text, both found only in source. Edison's documentation names no corpus for this agent. The 175 million papers, trials and patents mentioned in Edison's docs belong to the Literature agent and are not carried over.

**Elicit Reports** is filed on its Research Gap Analysis template. This is one of the templates the help centre offers when you start a Report, and a release note says it "identifies where current evidence falls short". It has a row apart from Elicit's dual-review entry in Human Approval because a different module answers for it. A Systematic Review's Setup page also has a report template setting, though that article names no templates, and this row rests on the Report workflow alone. A Report runs Elicit's own rapid review: it searches Elicit's corpus, sets screening criteria from your question, screens what it found, extracts data and writes the report. The help centre says the Research Agent cannot launch one, so the row is named for Reports. You supply the question, the template and the depth. The next act the help article documents is reviewing the finished report, and Elicit lists "A very rough draft of a related works section" among a report's uses. Editing the screening criteria or screening papers in or out is optional, and the help article offers it on the Pro, Scale and Enterprise plans. No documented step asks anyone to rule on a single gap. Bench fails its first clause, since what the help article describes it handing back is a report rather than a setup to run. Delegation then matches: a question goes in, a finished document comes out, and the exports the help article lists carry Elicit's own screening reasoning with no field it names for who decided. The entry meets the rule for end-to-end generators on its trace, because the help article has the reader click within the report to review supporting quotes and reasoning. A reader can check the evidence behind a stated gap more readily than the gap itself. The per-step CSVs list every paper found, each paper's screening result with Elicit's reasoning, and the extraction table with its supporting quotes, and the PDF or Word export numbers its citations in line. A gap claims that something is missing, and these exports show which papers were screened in and why others were excluded. None of the public pages checked describes the template's sections or how it decides that evidence falls short. The entry therefore rests on that release-note clause and on the template's name in the how-to.

**Arbor idea-check** is one command of Arbor, an Apache-2.0 system from the Gaoling School of Artificial Intelligence at Renmin University of China and Microsoft Research. Arbor's main loop, which takes a codebase and a goal through its own experiments to a report, is not listed. The rule for end-to-end generators does not exclude the product, since by default the run plan it stages waits for an explicit confirmation before it launches. The row rests on the standalone `arbor idea-check`, which searches arXiv papers through the public alphaXiv API for one idea the researcher types and labels it novel, partial-overlap or prior-art-exists. The researcher rules on the idea and an optional focus, then on whether to believe the label, and the documentation describes no stop in between. What a reader can check is the list of closest papers, so the label can be held against the papers it names. The documentation promises those papers, and the code prints each with a URL and a one-line reason, as the model returned them. The only safeguard on those URLs found in the code is a prompt line telling the model to quote them as visited. When the search finds nothing useful or every page visit fails, the prompt tells the model to return no papers with the label novel and to say in the overlap risks that the search came up empty, so, if the model follows it, such a search shows a NOVEL badge with that note beneath it. The URL and reason format, the URL safeguard and the empty-search behaviour are all found only in source. The command satisfies no category's written test. Bench fails because it proposes no setup and runs nothing. Delegation fails on its second clause, since what comes back is a verdict printed in the terminal rather than work handed in under the researcher's name. Standing Verdicts comes closest, because the label is a judgment on one item, the idea, and it counts unless the researcher discounts it. Its third clause fails all the same. The summary of prior work and the overlap risks are claims about other people's papers, and neither the documentation nor the code shows them pointing to a source or carrying a label saying the model wrote them. The prompt asks neither field to cite, while the prompt for Arbor's grounded-ideation lane, in the same file, asks for "[n] markers to cite sources", again a point found only in source. Human Approval fails because no ruling is tied to a person and the verdict leaves only as terminal output or JSON. Critique fails its second clause twice: much of what it returns is other people's papers, and a one-line idea for work not yet done is not work the researcher has already written or recorded. It is therefore filed in Delegation by elimination, as Polaris and NeuriCo are. Inside a run the same search can vet each new idea, but that setting is off by default and its verdict is advisory, so the in-run use does not change the row.

### Standing Verdicts

**Ai2 Asta** also has a report path, which returns prose rather than a structured set of results. It is filed on the search and ranking path, because that is the one that puts a judgment on each paper. On the report path alone it would sit closer to Delegation. Its AutoDiscovery module does a different job and has a row of its own, below.

**Consensus Research Gaps Matrix** has a row of its own, apart from Consensus, because it serves a different job and leaves the researcher a different thing to rule on. The help article describes a grid that lays the papers behind a Pro message or Deep review, or the papers in a saved Collection, across dimensions such as population, study method and outcome. Where a paper lands in that grid is the model's reading of what the paper covers, a value read out of it. The documentation shows no control for accepting or moving a placement, so the grid as built stands until a reader opens a cell and disagrees, and the module sits in Standing Verdicts for that reason. The written analysis around the grid can be exported as a document, and the documentation does not say whether the grid goes with it. Judged alone, as the module rule asks, this module hands back a grid. What the researcher rules on is whether a cell with little or no evidence is a real gap or an artefact of what was retrieved, and which cell, if any, deserves a further search. A reader can check which papers sit in each cell and why each was placed there, since the help article says a cell opens to the papers behind it and to why they were placed there, and the announcement says each paper opens with the passage behind its placement highlighted. The documentation found gives a reader no way to settle from the grid alone whether a thin cell is a real gap. The vendor's own announcement calls such a gap "not proof that no research exists". An empty cell is marked "Potential Gap", and the researcher can choose Investigate further with Deep, which "turns that row-by-column intersection into a focused search". The documentation found does not say whether that search, or anything in the grid itself, shows whether a gap has since been filled. The entry meets the bar for the second stage on what it leaves to check rather than on the gaps it names.

**SCiNiTO Gap Finding Agent** is filed on the agent SCiNiTO runs inside AI Chat on papers the researcher has selected. It returns a prioritized list of methodological, knowledge and technical gaps with an explanation of why each gap matters, along with impact and feasibility assessments and next steps. The researcher rules on which papers it reads and which gaps to pursue. Its help pages show no step to accept or reject a single gap, so each gap and the ranking by urgency, impact and feasibility stand as returned. It sits in Standing Verdicts because each gap is a claim about what the selected papers leave unaddressed, returned with the agent's own reason why it matters. SCiNiTO's FAQ says every claim in an AI Chat answer is linked to a citation, and that the citations come only from the selected papers when sources are selected. That trail is what a reader can check: open the cited paper and see whether it really leaves the gap open. The ranking is the model's own judgment, and no source can check it. The citation trail is documented for AI Chat answers in general, not on the agent's own page. SCiNiTO's agents overview places the Gap Finding Agent inside AI Chat, and the app code shows the gap action going through the same chat call, a point found only in source. Without that trail this module would be the ranked list that the second-stage bar allows only as a close alternative. The placement reads each gap as an item. An item, as [defined above](#three-words-the-tests-depend-on), is the unit the product invites you to accept or reject, and the help page invites the researcher to focus on some gaps without showing a control that accepts or rejects one, so on a strict reading the entry would fall to Delegation by elimination. SCiNiTO also has a Gap Finder in Research Spaces, which searches SCiNiTO's corpus to check whether anyone has answered each gap since. That is a different module. The Research Spaces agents page says some agents may be marked Early access or Coming soon without naming which, and nothing in this row rests on it.

**Ai2 Asta AutoDiscovery** has its own row because the researcher rules on different things there than on the search and ranking path the Ai2 Asta row is filed on. It starts from a dataset and a description of its context rather than from a question. LLM agents propose a hypothesis with a plan for testing it, write and run the analysis code, and score the result by how far it moves an LLM's belief in the hypothesis, and that score steers which branch the search grows next. The researcher sets the dataset, the context, an optional intent and the experiment budget, and on the self-hosted package also the model, and afterwards decides which tested hypotheses to follow up. Nothing documented waits on a person until the budget is spent, and whether a test counts is decided by an LLM reviewer agent, so it fails the Bench human-act clause as Dakota does. It fails Delegation's second clause because what comes back is a table of tested hypotheses that the documentation invites you to audit row by row, not a finished piece of work handed in under your name. It then matches Standing Verdicts. Each tested hypothesis carries a belief shift and a flag saying whether it counts as surprising, and these are the record for every row you never open. Each row opens to the hypothesis, the statistical analysis and the Python code behind it. Its verdicts concern hypotheses drawn from the researcher's own data and scored against an LLM's prior belief, and the documentation describes that LLM as "a stand-in for that well-read scientist". The fit is with the written clauses more than with the facet grid, because the evidence behind each verdict comes from a computation on that data, which on the grid sits closer to a run than to a reader. What a reader can check is the test itself. For every hypothesis the hosted app keeps the code, the statistics and the reviewer's validity check, and the PyPI release writes the same into its static HTML report, a point found only in source, so the code can be rerun on the same data. The surprisal score is different: it is an LLM's belief in the hypothesis before and after it reads the experiment's plan, analysis and review, and the documentation describes nothing outside that model to check it against. Ai2 calls the hosted app an experimental feature in early access, and its pages point both ways on who can sign in. New accounts are promised credits and the feature is called broadly available, while the footer of both Ai2 posts still sends readers to the selective Asta Preview program. The repository's design notes record that the app-wide enrollment gate was removed, and the run routes in its code ask only for sign-in, a point found only in source. Signing in was not attempted to confirm this. The row does not depend on that answer, because the open-source package comes from the repository its documentation calls "the software behind autodiscovery.allen.ai", one of the two addresses Ai2's blog links for trying the app, and it installs from PyPI with a plain `pip install asta-autodiscovery`. Ai2's blog says early access is "Extended through Dec. 31", and the app's own text, found only in source, says its credits are valid until December 31st, 2026, so the hosted half of the Access cell is re-tested after that date.

**HypoGeniC** is filed on its data-only path, the method its Quickstart script runs by default, a point found only in source, and not on HypoRefine, which adds hypotheses drawn from literature PDFs you supply. The researcher supplies labelled examples already split into training, test and validation files, writes the prompt templates the model runs on, and picks the model. After that no documented step waits on a person. The model drafts hypotheses, scores each by how often it predicts the training labels correctly with that hypothesis in its prompt, and drafts new ones from the examples the bank gets wrong. Once the bank is full, the code keeps the hypotheses with the highest reward, a score that adds an exploration bonus to accuracy, and drops the rest, a point found only in source. Bench fails its first and third clauses: a hypothesis is not a setting to run, and no documented step waits on a person between rounds, which is the Dakota case. Delegation fails because what comes back is a scored bank of hypotheses in a JSON file, not work handed in under the researcher's name. It is filed in Standing Verdicts on one reading of that category's third clause. Each hypothesis carries an accuracy that stands unless the researcher re-tests it, and each accuracy is computed on labelled examples from the researcher's own files. The reading treats those scores as the system's assertions, because the hypothesis texts themselves cite no source and carry no mark saying the model wrote them. If such a mark is required, no category's written test fits, and the entry would be filed in Delegation by elimination, as Polaris and NeuriCo are. Its Target is the researcher's own data rather than work other people did, which the widened description of this category covers. The bank is ranked by reward, and what lifts it above an idea generator that only ranks is a test against data. Its documentation says each hypothesis is scored against the researcher's own labels. Its code adds two things a reader can check: the saved bank lists, for each hypothesis, the training examples it predicted correctly, by their position in the seeded training sample and their label, and the Quickstart run then scores the bank on the test file, which generation never uses. The score comes from the model's own predictions with the hypothesis in hand, so a high accuracy shows that the model can use the hypothesis to predict the labels, which does not by itself show that the hypothesis holds. It is a frozen release. The last commit on its default branch, master, dates from July 2025. Its code and datasets remain downloadable and its README documents a local-model install, so it meets the activity rule's test for a frozen release on the Quickstart route the row rests on. Its scripts default to gpt-4o-mini, which only the code shows, and OpenAI lists GPT-4o mini (gpt-4o-mini-2024-07-18), the only snapshot under that name, for shutdown on January 14, 2027. The cost lookup in its OpenAI wrapper names only four models, so moving to any other OpenAI model would need an edit to that lookup, again a point found only in source. The entry is therefore due for a re-test on that date.

**Patsnap Eureka Novelty Search** is filed on the Novelty Search agent in Patsnap Eureka's IP line, not on the FTO, drafting or engineering agents beside it. You describe a technical idea, and the agent summarises it, splits it into technical features, searches patent and non-patent literature, ranks up to 100 patents, and labels each feature Disclosed, Obvious or Undisclosed for each of three references it preselects, or up to five you choose. What a reader can check is that table. Every label names the reference it was judged against, the help pages say you can see how each feature appears in the patent text, and an optional rationale explains each label. Patsnap's IP glossary says an invention "is considered to have an inventive step if it is not obvious to a person skilled in the art", so the Obvious label appears to reach past novelty into inventive step, and part of what the table answers is a patentability question. On the workflow its help article documents, you confirm the agent's summary of your idea, and Auto Run, if selected, then carries "all of the following sections" through to the report. Without Auto Run you can also edit the features and the search terms and codes, choose the references and change any label. With it the labels reach the report without anyone opening them, and without it the docs describe a review of the whole table but no step that accepts each label, which is why they stand. Delegation's second clause fails mainly because what comes back is working material, and also because the documented flow has the researcher confirm the agent's reading of the idea before any finished work exists, in the way Polaris's promotion approval comes first. The line between working material and finished work here is the one the list already draws between Undermind, whose report summary sits over a ranked and scored table, and Ai2 Asta's report path, which returns prose and would sit closer to Delegation. Patsnap's verdicts are per-reference labels in a table, and its report summarises the steps and the tables. One question could move it. The help article names a "final Novelty Assessment" among the outputs, and a [second help article](https://help.patsnap.com/hc/en-us/articles/35547523970077-Novelty-Search) on the agent introduces its list of the report's contents with "including". Patsnap's IP walkthrough calls the output "a structured comparison report", but the marketing page says the report summarizes "closest prior art, novelty risks, evidence gaps, and patentability considerations", and only that page says "Every conclusion traces to source". If that assessment is a prose argument whose claims do not point to sources, the third Standing Verdicts clause fails, no later category fits, and the entry would go to Delegation by elimination. The checkpoints may also not reach every user. The pricing page lists "Human-in-the-Loop" under "Everything in Searching Pro, plus:" on its enterprise plan, and Patsnap's guide for individual users says "Some advanced collaboration and export features are only available in enterprise environments", naming "Human-in-the-loop workflows" among them. Neither page says which steps of this workflow that covers, and the docs do not say whether Auto Run is on by default, so an individual user may hold fewer of these checkpoints than the row describes. It meets the rule for end-to-end generators on its trace, since each label is tied to a named reference whose text can be opened.

## Clusters

Some systems pass every test and still answer the same question for the same reader as one already listed: the same job, the same category, and the same thing handed in and handed back. The list groups them instead of turning them away. One featured system keeps its full row, its card on the chooser and its column in the comparison tables. Every other member of the cluster is one line under it, naming one documented difference and linking the page it rests on; scripts/lib/clusters.js keeps the exact words. Weights you can host yourself, set against a hosted service, make a different question, as does being free where nothing comparable is. A different front end does not, and neither does a different market for the same capability.

The featured system is chosen by four criteria, in this order:

1. How clearly its public documentation states what the human rules on and what record the system leaves. That is the question this list is organised around, so it decides first.
2. How much of a README row for it a reader can verify on public pages.
3. Backing and maintenance: an institution, company or publication behind it, recent releases, and more than one substantial contributor.
4. Adoption, in stars or users, only to break a tie.

When a system reached the list counts for nothing. When the weekly scan finds a new member for a cluster, it compares the newcomer with the featured system on these criteria in both directions and recommends. The maintainer decides, and a featured system that loses becomes one line in its own cluster. A system that answers a question no listed system answers still gets a row of its own.

Neither document describes a verdict on whether an idea has been done before, or the prior work behind one. None of the six steps SciPIP's app lists judges novelty or ranks the ideas †. MOOSE-Copilot ranks its hypotheses by "averaged LLM self-evaluation scores". Idea Tree's documentation says it "currently does not retrieve external sources, execute code, or conduct real experiments" and that its scores "are model assessments of supplied material, not measurements". ScienceDiscovery itself stays listed as a close alternative to Wisp Science. None of the four answers the same question as a listed entry in stages 2 and 3, so none was added as a close alternative there. |
| [Paperguide](https://paperguide.ai/) | Its Research Gap Finder page describes extra searches "trying to DISPROVE each candidate gap", the kind of check the bar for gap finders asks for. Its writer's document settings also include an "AI Humanizer" option †. One of those guides also says "Neither result alone proves misconduct or determines authorship." Excluded on the criterion that excluded SciSpace. |

Out under the rule for grant, proposal and study-plan writers, which applies "even when they search the literature" and which removed Granted AI and Question First. |
